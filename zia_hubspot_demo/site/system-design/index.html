Executive summary

What this is · why it exists · how it was built · what it delivers

What this document is

A complete system design for a HubSpot CRM, covering the data model, sales and delivery pipelines, automation, marketing operations, customer experience and reporting. It is written for Lumen Talent Group, a talent-solutions firm that places embedded remote professionals with client organizations and runs a leadership education program alongside it.

This is a design document rather than a sales proposal. Every significant decision is stated with the reasoning behind it, the constraints are named openly, and anything that needs confirming against a live portal before it can be built is marked as such.

Why this work was undertaken

Lumen's revenue is almost entirely recurring. Clients pay hourly for people embedded in their teams, month after month, often for a year or more. A standard CRM records the moment a contract is signed and very little afterward — one deal, one amount, one close date.

The consequence is that the largest part of the business is invisible to the system meant to manage it. Current recurring revenue cannot be stated without building a spreadsheet. Margin per engagement cannot be calculated at all. And an engagement that is quietly deteriorating produces no signal until the client cancels, by which point the revenue has already gone.

A second problem sits alongside it. Lumen sells to client organizations and separately recruits the talent it places — two entirely different audiences sharing one contact database with nothing structural separating them. Without a fix, candidates receive client marketing, lead counts are inflated by applicants, and the team stops trusting the data.

How the design was approached

The method was to begin with how the business actually operates rather than with what the software happens to offer. Three questions shaped everything that followed: what does the company actually sell, what does it deliver afterward, and which of those two does the current system fail to record?

The work then ran in a deliberate order. The object model came first, because pipelines and automation built on the wrong data model have to be torn down and rebuilt. Then the pipelines and the entry requirements at each stage, since those determine what data ever reaches the system at all. Then the automation, and only afterward the reporting — a dashboard can never be better than the model underneath it.

Where the company already had established vocabulary and a defined process, that language was adopted rather than replaced. A CRM that speaks the team's own words gets used. One that imposes unfamiliar terminology gets worked around, and then quietly abandoned.

What the design delivers

  • A Placement record that exists independently of the deal that created it, carrying its own rate, hours, start and end dates, and health score. This single change is what makes recurring revenue, gross margin and churn reportable.
  • A contact type discriminator separating clients from talent at record level, so the two audiences cannot contaminate each other's lists, campaigns or reports.
  • Three purpose-built pipelines — client acquisition, delivery, and talent sourcing — each with required fields at the stages where that information matters, so data is captured at the moment people are motivated to provide it.
  • Nine automated workflows, the central one replacing roughly a day of manual coordination at every new placement with a single trigger that moves six departments at once.
  • An attribution model that traces marketing spend through to recurring placement revenue rather than stopping at the booking amount, which for this business understates channel return by a wide margin.
  • A reporting layer in which every executive metric states the formula behind it, so disagreements happen over definitions before anything is built rather than over numbers afterward.

How it helps

Four things become possible that are not possible today.

Leadership can see the business. Recurring revenue, margin by tier and by client segment, and the total value of the live book become standing figures on a dashboard rather than a monthly reconstruction exercise.

Deteriorating engagements surface early. A nightly health score built from check-in recency, satisfaction trend, escalation volume and scope drift produces a ranked list of the placements needing attention this week — weeks before a cancellation would otherwise arrive.

The team stops doing work the system can do. The coordination chain at each new placement runs automatically and identically every time, including the steps people currently forget under pressure.

Decisions get better inputs. Qualification runs against explicit weighted criteria rather than individual judgment. Marketing spend is measured against revenue actually delivered. Forecasting reports both new bookings and the existing recurring base, which is the question leadership is really asking.

01

The business, before any architecture

3 min

Context — everything downstream is a consequence of this

Lumen sells embedded remote talent in three tiers, runs a leadership education arm, and does organizational-development consulting around both. That shape is unusual, and it is the reason an off-the-shelf HubSpot build won't hold.

TIER 01

Core

$12/hr starting

Focused execution — coordinators, scheduling, customer service, records, billing support.

TIER 02 · MOST COMMON

Momentum

$16/hr starting

Cross-functional contributor — marketing, HR ops, sales support, senior EA. Driving outcomes in 30–60 days.

TIER 03

Summit

$22/hr starting

Strategic operator — talent acquisition lead, director-level ops, chief of staff, org development lead.

Three structural facts drive every decision that follows.

One — two-sided business. Clients are one audience. The talent placed is a completely different audience with its own funnel, vetting and qualification. Both live in one CRM.

Two — the sale is a moment, the delivery is a subscription. A client signs once. The embed then runs for months, bills hourly against a weekly commitment, and can end without the deal record ever changing.

Three — the relationship doesn't end at placement. Ongoing check-ins, continuity planning, LumenU access for placed talent. That's a post-sale process with its own cadence and its own failure modes.

One more thing the client list makes obvious: Lumen's book is concentrated in private-practice healthcare — dental groups, DSOs, physical therapy — with speakers and professional services at the edges. That's a segmentation dimension the CRM should carry as a first-class property, not something you reconstruct by reading company names.

02

Where a standard HubSpot build breaks

4 min

The audit findings this design answers

Break 01 — One contact table, two audiences

Candidates and clients share a database with nothing separating them

HubSpot gives you one Contact object. Without a hard type discriminator, a placed coordinator and a prospective dental group owner sit side by side in every list, workflow and report.

What you feel: a candidate receives a "book a 15-minute alignment call" nurture. Lead counts inflated by applicants. Nobody trusts the database enough to send from it.

Break 02 — The deal closes once, the revenue recurs

Forecasting reports a signature, not a book of business

A Deal carries one amount and one close date. A Momentum embed at $16/hr for 40 hours over fourteen months is fifty-plus billing periods, and a mid-engagement tier upgrade is a change to a live thing — not a new sale.

What you feel: the pipeline report and the bank account disagree. Nobody can state current recurring revenue without a spreadsheet.

Break 03 — Delivery has no home in the CRM

The most important record in the business doesn't exist

The placement — this person, embedded with that practice, at Momentum tier, 30 hours a week, since March — is the unit actually delivered. In most builds it lives in a project tool or a sheet, and is therefore absent from every leadership dashboard.

What you feel: you know how many deals closed. You cannot say how many people are embedded, at what utilization, at what margin, or which engagements are wobbling.

Break 04 — No attribution, so marketing spend is a guess

You can count leads but not what produced revenue

Without UTM discipline, a defined conversion event and a contact-to-deal attribution path, marketing reports on form fills while leadership asks about closed revenue. The two never meet.

What you feel: "which channel should we spend more on?" is answered by intuition, and the answer changes depending on who you ask.

Break 05 — Education doesn't fit the sales shape

LumenU gets forced into deals, or abandoned entirely

A cohort has seats, dates, a facilitator and a completion rate, and enrollment is many-to-many. None of that is a pipeline stage — so it ends up in a spreadsheet and drops out of the customer picture.

What you feel: you can't see that a LumenU graduate at a client practice is the warmest talent lead you have.

Framing note: none of these are HubSpot's fault, and none require leaving HubSpot. Four are data-model problems and one is a measurement-discipline problem. All five are fixable inside the platform.
03

CRM architecture

6 min

Custom objects · properties · standardized data · adoption

Get the objects right and the workflows, pipelines and dashboards become obvious. Get them wrong and no amount of automation rescues it.

The one distinction to say out loud: Deal ≠ Placement

A Deal is the sale. A Placement is the delivery. Different lifespans, different owners, different metrics.

Standardizing data across departments

Every field that will ever appear in a report is a dropdown, never free text. Given a book concentrated in dental and physical therapy, practice_type is exactly where free text would bite — "Dental", "dental", "DSO" and "Dental Group" become four rows in every chart, and the segment analysis quietly becomes useless.

Lifecycle stage is workflow-owned, not human-owned: manual editing locked, stages move on evidence. That single setting is the difference between a funnel report that means something and one that reflects whoever last remembered to update a dropdown.

Adoption, which is the part most builds skip

A CRM nobody uses correctly is a very tidy database. Three things drive adoption more than training does: record layouts that put the five fields a rep actually needs at the top of the sidebar; stage gates that ask for data at the moment the rep is motivated to give it; and vocabulary drawn from how the team already speaks — Align, Define, Match, Embed rather than Qualified Lead and Proposal Sent.

Margin, which only exists once Placement does

With hourly_rate on the Placement and cost_rate on the Talent Profile, gross margin per embed becomes a calculated property — and so does margin by tier, by practice type, by consultant. No Deal-based model can answer that, and it's usually what a founder wants to know most.

The constraint I'd raise rather than hide: custom objects require an Enterprise subscription on at least one Hub. On Professional, build Placement as a second Deal pipeline — roughly 80% of this design survives, and the migration path stays clean.
04

Pipelines and lifecycle stages

4 min

Pipelines · lifecycle stages · stage gates

One generic pipeline forces three different processes into one set of stages, and none of them can then be measured. Three pipelines, each with entry requirements rather than decorative labels.

Client Acquisition — ALIGN → DEFINE → MATCH

Owned by sales. Measures conversion and velocity.

Alignment Call BookedRole Defined ⚑ Showcase ScheduledShowcase Delivered Agreement SentClosed WonClosed Lost ⚑

Stage gate ⚑ — cannot leave Role Defined without tier_requested, hours_per_week, practice_type and fit_score. Closed Lost requires disqualifier_reason. This is the highest-leverage control in the build: it collects the data the whole downstream system depends on, at the one moment the rep is motivated to provide it.

Placements (Delivery) — EMBED

Owned by customer success. Measures how fast a signature becomes real revenue.

Awaiting MatchShowcasingKickoff Scheduled Systems AccessWeek-1 Check-in30-Day Review Steady State ⚑Ended

Stage gate ⚑ — Steady State requires documentation_current and a first CSAT response. Stage probabilities stay at zero until Steady State, so this pipeline never pollutes the sales forecast — a small setting that prevents a large reporting problem.

Talent Sourcing

Owned by talent ops. Measures bench depth and time-to-ready.

AppliedScreenedAssessment Bench-Ready ⚑In ShowcasePlaced

Stage gate ⚑ — Bench-Ready requires tier_certified, timezone_band and availability_hrs. Nothing enters the matching pool without them, which is what makes automated matching possible at all.

Lifecycle stages, defined once

Lifecycle is a property on the contact, distinct from pipeline stage on the deal — conflating the two is one of the most common build errors. The definitions here are deliberately narrow so that everyone means the same thing: Subscriber (opted in, no intent), Lead (any conversion), MQL (fit criteria known and positive), SQL (alignment call booked), Opportunity (deal past Role Defined), Customer (at least one active placement), Evangelist (referred someone, tracked as such).

05

Automation & operations

6 min · live demo

Sophisticated workflows · lead routing · onboarding journeys · internal processes

The signature automation first, then the routing logic underneath it.

Today a Closed Won starts a chain of messages: a lookup for who's available, a manual project task, someone remembering the welcome email, someone else remembering the check-in. Here is that chain as one workflow.

Lead routing — three rules, in priority order

Routing is not one rule, it's a cascade. Speed matters more than perfect assignment: a lead answered in five minutes converts at a multiple of one answered the next day, so the design optimizes for time-to-first-touch and corrects ownership afterward if needed.

The other five workflows

  • Contact type enforcement — sets contact_type from form source at creation. Anything ambiguous becomes Unclassified and raises a task, so the gap is visible rather than silently defaulting.
  • Fit score routing — high scores skip to showcase, mid scores enter a nurture built around their two weakest criteria, low scores get a disqualify task with a reason required.
  • Check-in cadence — dated tasks at day 7, 30, 90 then quarterly, with CSAT at 30 and 180 days, and escalation when a check-in goes 14 days overdue.
  • Placement health scoring — nightly, across all active placements. Detail in §08.
  • LumenU enrollment — a placement reaching Steady State enrols the talent in the current cohort, closing the loop on the development promise.
A detail worth mentioning aloud: re-enrollment is off on the Match & Embed workflow. Without that, editing a won deal spawns duplicate placements. Catching it before it happens is the difference between a workflow and an architecture.
06

Marketing operations

6 min

Campaigns · landing pages · forms · nurture · attribution

As the architect I don't write the campaigns — I build the infrastructure that lets marketing run them at speed and prove what they returned. Attribution is the part that turns marketing from a cost line into a decision tool, so it gets the most space here.

Campaign architecture and naming

Every asset — email, landing page, form, list, workflow — carries a naming convention: TEAM | TYPE | SEGMENT | DATE | NAME. It reads as bureaucracy until the day someone asks which assets belong to the Q3 DSO push and the answer takes four seconds instead of an afternoon. Everything is attached to a HubSpot Campaign so spend, assets and influenced revenue roll up in one place.

Landing pages and forms

  • One page template per segment, not per campaign — dental group, solo practice, physical therapy, professional services. Content varies; structure doesn't. Marketing ships a new page in an hour rather than a week.
  • Three form types, deliberately different. The alignment-call booking asks qualification questions so the rep arrives half-informed. The showcase request is gated harder — tier interest and hours required, because that's a real buying signal. The talent application writes contact_type = Talent directly, so the two audiences separate at the point of entry rather than by cleanup later.
  • Progressive profiling so a returning visitor is asked something new instead of the same four fields. Fit criteria get collected across visits rather than in one intimidating form.
  • Hidden UTM fields on every form, mapped to first-touch and last-touch properties. This is the foundation attribution stands on, and it must exist before the first campaign runs — retrofitting it means the historical data is simply gone.

Nurture that responds to why someone isn't ready

Generic drip sequences perform poorly because they answer no specific objection. The fit scorecard gives us something better: we already know which criteria a prospect failed. If they haven't defined the role, they enter the role-definition track. If leadership isn't aligned on hybrid teams, they enter the change-management track. The nurture addresses the actual blocker, which is both more effective and more honest.

The attribution model, stated plainly

  • First touch answers "what makes people aware of us" — write it once at contact creation and never overwrite it. Judges top-of-funnel content and brand spend.
  • Last touch answers "what converts" — the source on the conversion that created the deal. Judges bottom-of-funnel offers.
  • Multi-touch answers "what contributed" — every interaction between, weighted. Useful for judging the middle, and the place to be most careful about over-claiming.
  • Placement-revenue attribution — the layer standard reporting misses. Roll recurring placement value back to the originating campaign so a channel is judged on the revenue it actually produced over fourteen months, not on one booking amount.

The honest limit, which I'd say out loud: attribution is directional, not accounting. Dark social, word of mouth in a tight dental community, and a conference conversation six months earlier will never appear in a UTM. The model is for deciding where the next marketing dollar goes — not for proving what caused a sale. Presenting it as certainty is how RevOps loses credibility with a founder who knows the real story behind a given client.

07

Sales operations

4 min

Pipeline optimization · forecasting · enablement · less admin

Forecasting, which needs two numbers not one

Most forecasts show weighted pipeline and stop. For a recurring-delivery business that's half the picture and the less important half. The forecast here has two components:

  • New bookings — weighted pipeline from the acquisition pipeline, using stage probabilities that were set from actual historical conversion rather than from optimism.
  • Existing recurring — the sum of active placement monthly value, minus a churn allowance derived from placements currently scored At Risk.

Reported together, they answer the question a founder actually has: if we sold nothing next quarter, what would we make — and what would put that at risk? No Deal-only model can produce that number.

Sales enablement, built as assets rather than advice

  • Playbooks per tier surfaced on the deal record — discovery questions for Core versus Summit are genuinely different conversations, and the rep shouldn't have to remember which.
  • Objection cards for the six disqualifiers. Every "not a fit" reason is a predictable objection with a known best answer. "We just need someone cheap" and "we only need cover for three months" each deserve a written, tested response rather than improvisation.
  • A snippet and template library so the twentieth follow-up email is as good as the first.
  • Documents with engagement tracking — knowing a proposal was opened four times by three people is a real buying signal and it costs nothing to capture.

Reducing admin, which is where reps get their time back

Meeting links replace scheduling threads. Quotes generate from deal properties rather than a Word template. Email and call logging is automatic. Required fields are collected by stage gates instead of by nagging. The measurable goal: a rep should be able to move a deal from alignment call to agreement without typing anything twice.

Customer experience

Onboarding · CS automation · feedback · tickets · client journeys

Onboarding, instrumented

The onboarding ticket set fires automatically at Closed Won: systems access, communication channels, payroll setup, role documentation. Each assigned, each dated relative to the embed start. The pipeline stage tracks progress and the elapsed time to Steady State becomes the Time-to-Value metric.

Placement health — where the money actually leaks

Placements don't churn on a date; they degrade for weeks first. A health_score recalculated nightly from four signals already sitting in the CRM:

  • Check-in recency — nobody has spoken to this practice in five weeks.
  • CSAT trend — satisfaction falling, not merely low.
  • Escalation volume — friction rising in delivery.
  • Scope drift — hours logged versus contracted; under-served, or ready for an upgrade.

Two thresholds, two automations. Below the floor: alert the CSM, task dated tomorrow, escalate to leadership if still red after seven days. Above the ceiling with positive scope drift: flag a tier-upgrade opportunity — Core at $12 moving to Momentum at $16 is the cheapest revenue in the business.

The claim I'd make carefully: this doesn't predict churn. It surfaces the placements a human should look at this week, ranked. A smaller promise than "AI churn prediction", and one the system can keep on day one.

Department dashboards

  • Sales — velocity by stage, conversion by practice type, showcase-to-close rate, speed-to-first-touch, stage-gate compliance.
  • Marketing — conversions by channel and campaign, form completion rates, nurture-track progression, first-touch to MQL.
  • Talent Ops — bench depth by tier and timezone, time-to-ready, showcase acceptance rate.
  • Customer Success — placements by health band, check-in compliance, SLA attainment, CSAT by consultant, documentation currency.

CRM health — the dashboard nobody looks at until something is wrong

Records missing required fields, workflows erroring in the last seven days, unassigned records, duplicate rate, properties with zero fill. This is the standing monitor that keeps the system from decaying quietly — and it's the same set of checks the audit tool runs, so week one and week fifty use the same definition of healthy.

Continuous improvement

3 min

Proactively find efficiency gains · reduce friction · recommend better ways

"Continuous improvement" is the one JD line that usually means nothing in practice, because nobody attaches a mechanism to it. Here's the mechanism.

A cadence, not an intention

  • Weekly — check the CRM health dashboard. Workflow errors and unassigned records get fixed the same week, not discovered in a quarterly review.
  • Monthly — one process review with one team. Rotate. The question is always the same: what did you do manually this month that you resented? That question has produced more good automation than any roadmap I've written.
  • Quarterly — re-run the portal audit and compare against the last one. Property fill rates trending down means a process is being skipped; stage stagnation moving means the pipeline no longer matches reality.
  • Annually — revisit the object model itself. Businesses change shape, and an architecture that isn't revisited becomes the thing people work around.

A visible backlog

Improvement requests go in one place with an owner, an estimate and an impact note — not into a Slack thread. Two consequences: the team can see their request wasn't ignored, and leadership can see the trade-offs being made rather than wondering what the CRM person does all week.

The audit, as a tool rather than an event

The portal audit that produces the week-one findings is a script, not a manual review — so re-running it costs minutes. That turns "audit the instance" from a one-off onboarding task into a quarterly health check, which is the difference between a system that's improved and one that was improved once.

Most teams don't fail because of effort. They fail because roles blur, decisions stall, and leaders carry too much.

That's true of CRMs too. A system where ownership is ambiguous and required fields are optional produces exactly that failure. Every stage gate, every routing rule and every named owner in this design exists to prevent it.

11

Where AI earns a place — and where it doesn't

1 min

AI workflow automation

Worth doing: ranking bench candidates for a showcase and drafting the reasoning for the top three, so talent ops reviews a shortlist instead of assembling one. Summarising a placement's activity into a check-in brief before a client call. Turning open-text CSAT responses into themes that feed the health score.

Not worth doing: letting a model set lifecycle stages, score fit unsupervised, or write anything a client receives without review. That's exactly where a confident wrong answer quietly corrupts the reporting the whole system exists to produce.

The pattern throughout: AI proposes, a human commits, the CRM records who committed it.

Audit · recommend · implement · dashboards · reduce manual work

Days 1–30AUDIT

Find out what's actually there

  • Run the portal audit — property fill rates, free-text fields that should be constrained, unowned records, stalled deals, workflow errors. Findings by day two, not week four.
  • Stakeholder interviews across sales, marketing, talent ops, CS and education: what do you do manually every week that you resent?
  • Read the existing playbook and build against your spec first, then say what it doesn't yet cover.
  • Ship two quick wins anyway, so month one produces something you can feel.
Days 31–60ARCHITECT

Agree the model, then build it

  • Present findings and a prioritized roadmap, including what I recommend not doing.
  • Build the object model and back-fill existing records cleanly.
  • Rebuild the pipelines with stage gates; lock lifecycle stage to workflows.
  • Fit scorecard and lead routing live, with speed-to-lead measured from day one.
  • UTM and attribution plumbing — this has to exist before the next campaign, because it can't be retrofitted.
Days 61–90AUTOMATE

Turn on the engine and hand over the keys

  • Match & Embed live, plus the integration and onboarding ticket set.
  • Check-in cadence and health scoring running nightly with alerts routed to named owners.
  • Executive dashboard plus four department dashboards, every definition written down and agreed.
  • Documentation and enablement — a session per team, because adoption is the difference between a system and a very tidy database.
  • Set the improvement cadence from §10 so this doesn't decay the moment the build finishes.
13

What I'd need to ask you

2 min

The diagnostic questions behind the design

This design is built from public materials and a job description. Six answers would confirm it or change it, and I'd rather find out now than in week three.

Which HubSpot subscription are you on today?

Why it matters: custom objects need Enterprise. On Professional the model becomes a second-pipeline interim — better to say that up front than design something unbuildable.

Where does a placement live right now — a project tool, a sheet, or somebody's head?

Why it matters: sets migration scope, and tells me whether that tool is something I integrate with or replace.

What's in the priority automations playbook the JD mentions?

Why it matters: you already know what hurts. I'd rather build your list first and earn the right to propose mine.

Are candidates in HubSpot today, or in a separate ATS?

Why it matters: the single biggest fork in the data model. Both are workable; they're different builds.

Do forms currently capture UTM parameters?

Why it matters: attribution can only start from the day this exists. If it doesn't, that's day-one work and the historical data is unrecoverable.

What is the one number leadership currently cannot get without asking a person?

Why it matters: that's the first dashboard tile I build, whatever the rest of the roadmap says.

Talent on its own is static. What matters is talent in motion — aligned to the right structure, integrated into the right system, accountable to real outcomes. That's also a fair description of a CRM architecture. This is the same argument, applied to operations.

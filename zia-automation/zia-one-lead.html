Now follow Dana

Dana runs learning and development at a logistics company. She has a problem — her team leaders are struggling — so she Googles, lands on ZIA's website, and fills in the contact form. Here is everything that happens next.

  1. Dana

    She fills in the form

    Name, work email, company, her role, and what she needs help with. She clicks submit and sees "thank you". From her side, that is the whole interaction.

    Hidden in that form are two fields she never sees. One marks her as a client rather than a consultant. The other records that she came from the website rather than a spreadsheet import.

  2. HubSpot

    A contact record appears

    HubSpot creates a person record with everything she typed. At this moment she is a name floating on her own — not connected to any company, with nobody responsible for her, and nothing telling anyone she exists.

    This is where most CRMs quietly leak money.

  3. Automation · WF-08

    The system works out who she is and tells someone

    Four things happen without a human touching anything:

    It finds her company. Her email ends @brightpathlogistics.com, so the system looks for a company with that web address. If it exists, she is attached to it. If not, the company is created first, then she is attached. (It ignores gmail and the like — those tell you nothing about an employer.)

    It gives her an owner so a named person is accountable. It marks her as a new lead. And it raises a high-priority ticket — a job on someone's list saying "new enquiry, respond within 24 hours."

    If nobody touches that ticket within 24 hours, the system marks it as an SLA breach so it shows up on the dashboard as a failure rather than quietly ageing.

    Without this step: Dana sits in the database unattached and unassigned. She does not appear on any company report. Nobody is told. She waits, hears nothing, and buys from someone else — and no report anywhere shows that it happened.
  4. Salesperson

    A conversation, then a deal and a quote

    Someone calls Dana. She needs help with team leaders across three departments — that is a Momentum sized problem. A deal is created for roughly $18,000, and a quote is built from the price list rather than typed from memory.

    That last part matters. Because the price comes from actual line items, ZIA can later answer "are we discounting too hard on Momentum?" — 578 of the 1,500 line items in this portal carry a discount between 5% and 20%.

  5. HubSpot

    She says yes — Closed Won

    The deal moves to Closed Won. In most CRMs this is where the trail ends: the sale is recorded and everyone moves on. Here it is a starting gun.

  6. Automation · WF-03

    Delivery gets told automatically

    The system spots a won deal with no onboarding job attached, and creates one — linked to the deal, the company and to Dana. Confirm access, set the schedule, book the week-one checkpoint.

    Without this step: sales celebrates and delivery finds out late. The first experience Dana has after paying is being asked questions she already answered.
  7. Automation · WF-04 and WF-05

    A consultant is placed, and the work is watched

    A consultant from the network starts working with Dana's teams. The engagement gets a health score out of 100 — is it going well? The target is 70.

    If health drops into the 40s or 50s, the system raises a check-in job. If it falls below 40, it raises an urgent escalation and flags it as a breach. Nobody has to notice; the system notices.

    This is the finding. 144 engagements are currently scoring below 60. Only 22 are marked "At Risk". The score was being measured and looked at, but it never changed the status — so the CRM's own warning light was understating the problem roughly sixfold.
  8. Accounting → HubSpot → Automation · WF-07

    The invoice, and chasing it

    The actual invoice is raised in the accounting system, not in HubSpot — that is deliberate. A CRM should not pretend to be a ledger.

    But the state of that invoice is copied back onto the deal: sent, due, paid, how many days outstanding. So anyone in HubSpot can see whether a client has paid, without opening the accounting software.

    Then the system watches those dates. Anything past due gets a chase job, with urgency scaled by how late it is.

    What that found: 86 of 115 unpaid invoices are more than 90 days overdue — $1.60M. That is not a chasing backlog. That is nobody chasing at all.

What the system is, in one sentence

HubSpot stores the facts. Our automation engine is the thing that notices — it wakes up, compares what should be true against what is true, and fills in the difference.

On a paid HubSpot plan, steps 3, 6, 7 and 8 would be built-in features called workflows. This account is free, so those return an error. We rebuilt them outside — and because ours checks the current state rather than reacting to a moment, missing a run costs nothing. It simply catches up next time.

What actually happened when we tested this

Everything above is built. To prove it, a real submission was posted to the live form — exactly what Dana's browser would send.

HubSpot returned success. It showed the thank-you message. And then no contact was created.

The lead was silently thrown away The portal holds 1,002 contacts. The free plan allows 1,000. Creating another returns 402 — cannot create another CONTACT, it has exceeded the limit of 1000.

The form does not tell the visitor. It does not tell the business. Dana sees "thank you, we'll be in touch", believes she has made contact, and waits for a call that can never come — because her record was never created. There is no error anywhere for anyone to find.

This is the most valuable thing the test produced, and it is worth saying in an interview exactly as it is: a full-looking front door that silently drops every lead is worse than no front door at all, because the business does not know it is losing anything. A capacity limit had become a silent data-loss bug.

The fix is to make room — the portal is 2 records over a hard ceiling — and then to monitor headroom so it can never happen unnoticed again.

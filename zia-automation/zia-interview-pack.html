The eight application questions

Q1

How many years of hands-on experience do you have working in HubSpot?

Your number — state it honestly Do not round up. If it is under three years, lead with the honest figure and immediately follow with scope, because scope is what they are really asking about.

"[X] years. Rather than lead with the number, let me tell you what I've actually built — most recently a complete portal restructure covering CRM architecture, commerce, service and reporting, which I can walk you through live."

If they press on the number, do not defend it. Say: "I'd rather you judge the work than the years — here's the portal."

Q2

Which HubSpot Hubs have you worked in?

"Sales Hub most deeply — deal pipelines, stages, products, line items and quotes. Service Hub for ticketing, pipelines and SLA logic. Operations-style work through the API — data quality, programmable automation and integration boundaries. Marketing Hub is my thinnest area: I've built and shipped forms, but not email campaigns or landing pages, because the portal I've been working in doesn't have Marketing Hub."

Naming Marketing as your weak area first is deliberate. It is the one gap in the JD you cannot cover, so say it before they find it, and immediately show what you did instead:

"Without Marketing Hub I couldn't use HubSpot's attribution reporting, so I built first-touch attribution as a data model instead — a governed source property on the contact, rolled onto the deal, reported as revenue by channel. It found that outbound has our highest win rate at 28.6% and the largest average deal at $35,175, while being the least-used channel — and that paid search has produced zero revenue across five deals."

Q3

Have you built automated workflows in HubSpot?

"Yes — eight of them, though with an important caveat I'd rather give you upfront. The portal runs on a free tier where the workflow API returns 403, so I built the automation externally against the CRM API rather than in the workflow builder. Functionally they're the same eight workflows: lead routing, lifecycle sync, onboarding tickets, health monitoring, SLA escalation, compliance holds, collections, and inbound lead processing."

Then make the design point, because it is the strongest thing you have:

"There's one deliberate difference. A HubSpot workflow is event-driven — it fires once when a record crosses a threshold, and if it misses the event, that record is missed permanently. Mine reconcile state: each rule asks 'which won deals have no onboarding ticket?' and creates only the missing ones. The first live run wrote 540 records. Running it again immediately wrote zero. It cannot double-write, and a missed run self-heals on the next pass."

If you have built native workflows elsewhere, say so here This answer is about this portal. If you have prior hands-on experience in the workflow builder itself, lead with that and use the above as the second half.
Q4

Have you designed or restructured a HubSpot CRM from the ground up?

"Yes — the current one twice over, because the first state of the data was unusable. I inherited a portal with 20,551 companies where the source file had 1,600. It had been imported twice, and the property used to match deals to companies was empty portal-wide, so the planned deal import would have matched zero records and auto-created roughly a thousand more duplicates on top."

"I deduplicated by domain, disambiguated names by city, populated the join key from the website field, then associated every deal to its company explicitly by ID at creation rather than relying on fuzzy matching. The result is 1,500 of 1,500 deals linked, zero orphans, and zero companies missing a domain."

Q6

Describe a time you improved a business process using HubSpot. What was the problem, what did you build, and what was the outcome?

This is the question that decides the interview. Lead with the health-governance story below — it is a business insight, not a technical one, and it is the kind of thing an architect is hired to find. Keep the other two in reserve.

Q7

How comfortable are you building reports and executive dashboards in HubSpot?

"Comfortable, and I'd rather show you than rate myself. I have an executive dashboard running live against the portal — pipeline, delivery health, consultant bench, receivables — and I can refresh it from HubSpot while we're talking."

Then make the point that separates you from someone who drags fields onto a chart:

"The part I care most about is filter correctness. In this portal, asking HubSpot for the win rate returns 58.7%. The real acquisition win rate is 30.1%. The difference is that delivery engagements also close as Won, and unless every revenue report excludes them, you're adding delivery to sales and every downstream number is wrong. A dashboard that is confidently wrong is worse than no dashboard."

Have these ready, all computed and live: 60-day average sales cycle, pipeline velocity of $37,469/day, and a $2.52M weighted forecast against $7.54M of raw open pipeline.

Q8

Rate your confidence designing scalable systems across Sales, Marketing, Operations and Customer Success, 1–5.

Your rating — but here is the honest arithmetic You have strong evidence in Sales, Operations and Customer Success, and thin evidence in Marketing. A 4 is defensible and honest. A 5 invites a Marketing Hub question you cannot win.

"Four. I'd be a five on Sales, Operations and Customer Success — I've architected all three in the same portal and they're running. Marketing is where I'd want support: I've built forms and attribution as a data model, but I haven't run email campaigns or nurture sequences at scale. I'd rather tell you that now than discover it in month two."

A candid 4 with a named gap reads as more senior than an unqualified 5. It also sets up the 90-day plan, where you say what you would do about it.

Three STAR stories

1 · The health score nobody was acting on

Lead with this one. Business insight, not plumbing.

Situation
A consulting firm scoring every client engagement for health out of 100, against a target of 70. The score was captured on every engagement and shown on dashboards.
Task
Restructure reporting so leadership could see delivery risk. The brief was reporting; the problem turned out to be governance.
Action
Once the data was clean enough to cross-reference, I compared health score against the At Risk status field and found they had no relationship. The score was measured and displayed but never governed the status. I built two reconciling rules: one raising a check-in for engagements scoring 40–69, one raising a high-priority escalation below 40 and flagging an SLA breach. I also built a saved segment so the list is visible in the UI, not only in a report.
Result
144 engagements were scoring below 60. Only 22 carried the At Risk status — the CRM's own risk flag was understating exposure roughly sixfold. HubSpot's own filter engine independently returns 144 for that segment, so the number is verifiable in the UI without trusting my code.

2 · The import that would have silently failed

Data architecture and the discipline of checking first.

Situation
A portal holding 20,551 companies where the source file contained about 1,600. Timestamps showed the same file imported twice, three minutes apart. A 1,500-record deal import was scheduled next.
Task
Get the deals in, associated correctly, without making the duplication worse.
Action
Before importing I ran one search for a sample company by the domain the deal file matched on. It returned zero results — the domain property was empty portal-wide, because an earlier fix had only been applied to the deal files. I deduplicated companies by domain, disambiguated names by city, backfilled the domain from the website field, and then associated deals to companies explicitly by ID at creation rather than relying on HubSpot's matching.
Result
1,500 of 1,500 deals associated, zero orphans, zero companies missing a domain. Had it run as planned it would have matched nothing and auto-created roughly 994 further duplicate companies. One search, run before the import, was the difference between a ten-minute fix and a rebuild.

3 · The dry run that caught 817 duplicates

Engineering rigour. Use if they probe on testing or reliability.

Situation
Building automation to create an onboarding ticket for every closed-won deal without ever duplicating one.
Task
Guarantee the rule was safe to run repeatedly against live production data.
Action
I built a dry-run mode that evaluates and reports without writing. It printed alreadyCovered: 500 and wouldWrite: 817 in the same run — an impossible pair. The cause: HubSpot returns association IDs as numbers while object IDs everywhere else are strings, so every deduplication check silently missed. I fixed it once in the shared API client by coercing to string at the boundary, so no rule can reintroduce it.
Result
817 duplicate tickets prevented. The first live run created 317 legitimate tickets; the immediate re-run created zero. That idempotency became the standard every subsequent rule had to meet — and it caught two further bugs later, including one of my own.

Reserve · The front door that threw leads away

Use if asked about testing, monitoring, or "what would you fix first".

Situation
Building web forms so enquiries could enter the CRM instead of arriving by spreadsheet.
Action
Rather than assume the form worked, I posted a real submission end to end. HubSpot returned 200 and rendered the thank-you message — and created no contact. A direct API call revealed why: 402 — cannot create another CONTACT, it has exceeded the limit of 1000.
Result
A capacity ceiling had become a silent data-loss bug. The visitor is told they've made contact, the business is told nothing, and no error appears in any report. I freed headroom and added contact headroom to the health endpoint so it can never happen unnoticed again. A front door that looks complete and silently discards every lead is worse than no front door, because nobody knows they're losing anything.

The 30-60-90 day plan

The JD lists these as the first-90-day expectations. You have an unusual advantage: you can describe each phase from having actually done it, not hypothetically.

Audit

Days 1–30

  • Probe the tier and scopes before anything else. Distinguish what's missing because of a permission, a licence, or a product boundary — they all look identical from the outside.
  • Data quality baseline: duplicates, empty join keys, free-text fields that should be dropdowns, records with no associations.
  • Interview each team on where they leave HubSpot to get work done. That's where the automation gaps are.
  • Map the lifecycle end to end — first touch to renewal — and mark every handoff where data currently gets lost.
  • Deliverable: a written audit naming each defect, its business consequence, and the evidence.

Fix and prove

Days 31–60

  • Present findings ranked by revenue impact, not by effort.
  • Quick wins first: governed dropdowns, deduplication, required fields, correcting reports whose filters make them wrong.
  • Ship the priority automations from the playbook, each one dry-run before it writes, and each safe to re-run.
  • Executive dashboard with correct filters, plus the metrics leadership actually asks for: velocity, cycle time, weighted forecast, receivables.
  • Deliverable: automation live, dashboards trusted, every number traceable to a query.

Scale and hand over

Days 61–90

  • Close the lifecycle loops — onboarding, health, renewal, and the sales-to-delivery handoff.
  • Integration boundaries for anything that isn't the CRM's job — billing, project delivery, ClickUp — with state mirrored back so reporting stays in one place.
  • Monitoring: a health check that fails loudly on drift, breach, or a capacity ceiling. Assume nobody is watching.
  • Documentation and enablement so the system survives me — naming conventions, what each rule does, how to add a property safely.
  • Deliverable: a system somebody else can operate and extend.

The line that lands: "Every CRM is clean on launch day. What keeps it clean is convention and a reconciliation job, not discipline — because discipline doesn't scale and people are busy."

Questions to ask them

Generic questions get generic answers. These are diagnostic — each one tells you something about the job you'd actually be doing, and signals that you have done this before.

  1. What's your portal's tier, and which scopes are on the private app? Opens with the exact question a real audit starts on. It also tells you immediately how much of the JD is achievable.
  2. When a deal closes won, what happens next — and how much of it is somebody remembering to do it? The sales-to-delivery handoff is where consulting firms leak. Their answer tells you whether onboarding is automated or heroic.
  3. Is there a number on a dashboard that you don't fully trust? Almost always yes, and it's usually a filter problem. It sets you up to talk about the 58.7%-versus-30.1% win rate without forcing it.
  4. Which of your reports would break if someone typed a new value into a free-text field tomorrow? Tests whether they've thought about governance, and shows you have.
  5. How do you currently know a client engagement is going badly — before the client tells you? For an organizational-development firm this is the whole business. It leads straight into your strongest story.
  6. The JD mentions ClickUp. What's the boundary between it and HubSpot today, and where does the same data live in both? Shows you think in integration boundaries and duplicated sources of truth, not in tools.
  7. Am I walking into a system that needs scaling, or one that needs cleaning first? Ask it last. Their answer determines which 30-day plan you'd actually run.
